The Diagnosis

Where Consulting Engagements Usually Go Wrong

Most consulting and branding engagements start in the wrong place: a new identity, a campaign, a positioning statement. They start there because it's the visible, deliverable-friendly part of the work, and because the firms selling it have rarely run a clinic.

Meanwhile the real leaks go untouched: intake bottlenecks, referral drop-off, a scheduling system that creates a bad first impression before a patient sees a provider, documentation that eats hours a physician doesn't have. The rebrand launches, activity lifts for a quarter, then the same operational drag reasserts itself. The budget is gone and the organization is back where it started, with less appetite to try again.

National survey data backs this pattern up: a 2026 Harris Poll survey for Google Cloud found clinicians spend close to 28 hours a week on administrative tasks, and 82 percent report burnout symptoms tied to that load. Fix the workflow and you're also treating a real cause of the turnover problem next door.

The Reframe

Healthcare Engineering, Defined

Healthcare Engineering is our term for doing this work in the right order: structure first, an audit-plus-branding exercise never.

Phase 1

Diagnostic Audit

Stakeholder interviews across clinical, administrative, and ownership perspectives, paired with a direct review of workflows, scheduling, referral paths, and documentation load.

Phase 2

Root-Cause Mapping

The findings get traced back to their origin, not their symptom: distinguishing, for example, a staffing shortage that's a scheduling-design problem from one that's genuinely a hiring problem.

Phase 3

Structural Rebuild

We rebuild the operational pieces the diagnosis identified, in the sequence that prevents the fix from being undone by whatever caused the original problem.

Phase 4

Brand & Growth Strategy

Only now does external-facing work begin — positioning, messaging, and growth strategy built on a structure that can support the claims being made.

Phases 1 and 2 draw on the Human & Behavioral Diagnostics and Analytical & Data-Driven Rigor pieces of our method. Phase 3 onward is Business Strategy & Execution taking over. The end point isn't a new brand: it's fewer of the resignations, referral losses, and margin leaks that made the old one look necessary.

Technology & AI Adoption

Don't Buy Software You'll Abandon in a Year

Healthcare organizations get pitched new software and AI tools constantly, and most of that pitch happens without anyone checking whether the tool solves a problem you've diagnosed, or just one a vendor is selling. We evaluate new technology, AI included, against the specific operational gaps our diagnostic work has already identified, not against a features list. That means checking integration risk (does it fit your existing systems, or fight them), building a realistic adoption plan for the staff who'll use it day to day, and giving a straight answer on whether the problem is a technology problem at all, since a scheduling tool can't fix a scheduling policy nobody follows.

Independent research backs up why that check matters. KLAS Research, which rates healthcare IT independently of the vendors it covers, found only 38 percent of organizations reported their most recent major health-IT implementation fully met expectations. The gap is rarely the software itself. It's a mismatch between what got bought and what the organization needed, which is exactly the evaluation step a vendor's own sales cycle isn't built to provide.

Biomedical & Engineering Consulting

The Second Opinion Before You Sign a Seven-Figure Purchase Order

When a hospital buys major equipment (an ultrasound machine, an imaging system, any large capital or technology decision) the sales process usually runs directly through the in-house biomedical engineering or technical team. That's not a flaw in any individual's judgment. It's a structural problem: in-house engineers mostly see the products their usual vendors bring them, sales representatives have every incentive to build a close relationship with whoever signs off, and there's rarely time or budget to independently evaluate every competing option down to the datasheet.

This is a documented pattern, not a guess. A 2021 National Bureau of Economic Research study found that 87 percent of medical device sales in its hospital sample occurred at facilities where a clinician involved in the decision had received a payment from the device manufacturer. That doesn't mean the decision was wrong. It means the decision wasn't independent, and the hospital paying for the equipment usually has no way to tell the difference from outside the relationship.
